Reading the RULA Report Results

A completed RULA report is more than a single number. The score tells you how urgently a posture needs attention; the rest of the report tells you why, and that is what you need in order to change anything. This article walks through each tab and explains how to trace a score back to the posture that produced it.

1 Report structure

The RULA report has three tabs, matching the other assessment methods:

TabWhat it gives you
Score OverviewThe completed assessment: every component score, the scoring tables, and the final result. The equivalent of a filled-in RULA worksheet.
AnalysisHow the score behaves over time, so you can find the moments and postures driving it.
FindingsYour own written observations and recommendations.

If you created more than one posture task, each is reported separately and you switch between them within the report.

2 Score Overview

The Score Overview reproduces the structure of the RULA method itself, top to bottom. It opens with the general information for the assessment, then works through the two posture groups before combining them.

The layout follows the method's own two-group split:

GroupModules shown
Group A — arm and wristUpper Arm Position, Lower Arm Position, Wrist Position, Wrist Twist, then the Wrist & Arm total, muscle use and force/load, and Table A.
Group B — neck, trunk, legsNeck Position, Trunk Position, Legs, then the Neck/Trunk/Legs total, its own muscle use and force/load, and Table B.
CombinedTable C, the total score, and the score legend showing which action level applies.
Screenshot
RULA Score Overview tab

The full overview showing both posture groups, the three tables and the final score — the RULA equivalent of the NIOSH score overview screenshot.

Note that muscle use and force/load appear twice, once per group. That is not a display duplication — the method genuinely adds those points to both totals, which is why a change to the load weight can move the final score by more than one point.

3 Reading a posture module

Each posture module follows the same pattern, and learning to read one means you can read all of them. A module shows:

  • A row of reference figures, one per scoring band, with the angle range and the points it scores.
  • The band matching this task's peak frame is highlighted; the rest are greyed out.
  • Any adjustments that applied — twisting, side bending, abduction, a raised shoulder, arm support — listed underneath with their points.
  • The module's total score on the right.

This is the fastest way to see what drove a score. If the Trunk module highlights the 20–60° figure and shows a twist adjustment, you know immediately that the worker was bent forward and rotated at the peak frame — and that straightening the work presentation would address both.

Screenshot
A single posture module, close up

One module — Trunk Position is a good example — showing the four reference figures with one highlighted, the adjustment rows beneath, and the score badge.

Two figures can be highlighted at once

In the Lower Arm module the first two figures are elbow flexion bands, while the third shows the separate across-midline condition. When the hand works away from the body, that third figure lights up alongside the flexion band — they describe independent things, and both can apply.

4 The scoring tables

Tables A, B and C are shown in full, with the row and column for this task highlighted so you can see exactly which cell was used. This matters more than it might sound: because Table C is a lookup rather than a sum, seeing the cell tells you how much headroom there is.

If your Table A total sits at the edge of a band, a one-point improvement will drop the final score. If it sits in the middle, the same improvement may change nothing — and the tables let you check before you spend money on an intervention.

The tables clamp at the top end

Table A totals of 8 or more, and Table B totals of 7 or more, are clamped to the last row and column. Beyond that point the method stops distinguishing — a task there already requires immediate investigation, so a worse posture cannot make the recommendation any more urgent.

5 Analysis

The Score Overview describes a single frame — the worst one. The Analysis tab shows the whole task, which is where you find out whether that peak was a brief transient or a posture the worker holds continuously. The distinction matters enormously for what you do about it.

Score per frame

A timeline of the RULA score across the task. A flat line near the peak means a sustained posture; a single spike means a transient. Both can produce the same peak score and call for entirely different responses.

Frame insights

Select any frame to see the component values behind it. This answers "why was the score high here" for any moment, not just the peak.

Body segments and variables

The underlying joint angles extracted from the recording, so you can inspect the measurement itself rather than only the score derived from it.

6 Critical Moments

Critical moments are the frames worth showing someone else. Motionprint Ergo detects them automatically using two strategies:

  • Peak detection — the single highest-scoring frame.
  • Sustained risk detection — the window with the highest average score over at least one second.

Those two rarely coincide, and the difference is informative. A peak without a sustained window is a transient; a sustained window scoring almost as high as the peak is a posture the worker lives in.

You can edit the auto-detected moments, adjust their frame ranges, add a description, or create your own. If you would rather work entirely manually, turn off Auto-generated critical moments in the settings.

7 Findings

The Findings tab is where your professional judgement goes. It provides five sections by default:

  • Current Situation
  • Observations
  • Risk Assessment
  • Recommendations
  • Conclusion

The sections can be renamed, reordered or removed, and the tab itself can be disabled if you do not use it. Those defaults live in your RULA report settings, so you set them once rather than per report.

The score is a screening result, not a conclusion

RULA flags a posture as needing attention. It does not identify the cause, weigh the cost of fixing it, or account for anything outside its scope. The Findings tab exists because that reasoning is yours to supply — and a report that reaches a reader without it is much easier to misread.

8 Export and saving

A finished report can be exported to PDF for sharing or Excel for further analysis, and saved to the Projects Database so it can be reopened later with its settings intact.

Filename templates, company branding and whether only the averaged posture task is exported are all controlled in the settings.

Next steps: to adjust the defaults behind all of this, see RULA Report Settings. For the method itself, see Understanding the RULA Assessment.
